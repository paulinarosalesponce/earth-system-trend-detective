How to be a trend detective

Earth science data is enormous, and a variable can rise in one place while falling in another — even when the same process drives both. This tool narrows that down to one question you can actually answer: is this variable, in this place, moving in a consistent direction?

  1. Pick a variable. Start with a theme, then a variable. Each one has a plain-language definition.
  2. Pick a place. Click a point, or drag a box to compare many grid cells at once.
  3. Read the verdict. You get the rate of change, the total change, and whether three independent statistical tests call it significant.

Not finding a significant trend is a real result too. Sometimes a trend just needs a longer record to emerge.

Time series

Data: NASA POWER (Prediction Of Worldwide Energy Resources), monthly time series, 1981–present, fetched live from power.larc.nasa.gov. POWER's meteorological variables are derived from NASA's MERRA-2 reanalysis and its radiation variables from NASA GEWEX/CERES; grid resolution is 0.5° latitude × 0.625° longitude. Variable definitions come from the POWER parameter catalogue.

Map reference data: coastlines and country borders from Natural Earth (public domain); town and city names from GeoNames (CC BY 4.0), limited to places above about 20,000 people. These are drawn for orientation only and are embedded in this page, so the map makes no request to any server other than NASA POWER.

Please note: this tool fits linear trends. Real Earth system change can be non-linear, can shift abruptly, and can be influenced by cycles such as ENSO that are not removed here. A significant linear trend is evidence of consistent directional change over the period you chose — not a forecast, and not proof of a cause.
