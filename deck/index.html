The problem

Detecting change in Earth system data is harder than it looks

NASA has published decades of Earth system measurements and the data is freely available, yet relatively little of it is examined by anyone outside a research institution. One reason is simply the number of variables on offer, which makes it difficult to know which of them bears on a particular question.

A second reason is spatial. Because a variable can be increasing in one part of a region while it decreases in another, a single regional average may conceal two opposing signals that happen to cancel, and the resulting figure will describe neither of them.

There is also a statistical difficulty that is easy to overlook, which is that a sloping line fitted to a noisy record is not in itself evidence of change. Random variation produces sloping lines as a matter of course, so any claim of a trend has to be tested before it can be relied on.

What this currently involves
Answering a question of this kind today generally means registering for an Earthdata account, identifying the relevant collection among more than a hundred, downloading and subsetting the files, and then knowing enough statistics to recognise that monthly observations violate the independence assumption of the test most people would reach for first.

That is a weekend of work before the first answer appears, and in practice most people stop well before the statistics.

The method

The tool reduces the task to three steps, all carried out in a browser.

01

Choose a variable

Twenty-five variables are grouped by subject rather than by the instrument or model that produced them, since someone investigating drought is unlikely to know which product contains soil moisture. Each carries a definition in ordinary language, so that root-zone soil wetness is introduced as the water available to plants in the top metre of soil.

02

Choose a place

A location is given either as a single point or as a box of up to ten degrees on a side. Where a box is used, every NASA grid cell within it is analysed on its own rather than averaged into one figure, so that disagreement between neighbouring cells stays visible instead of cancelling out.

03

Read the verdict

The result appears first as a statement in ordinary language giving the variable, the place, the rate of change per decade and whether that change is statistically significant, with the full set of test statistics set out beneath it for anyone who wants to examine how the conclusion was reached.

DATA REQUESTED FROM NASA POWER AT THE MOMENT OF THE QUERY MONTHLY VALUES, 1981 TO PRESENT RUNS ENTIRELY IN THE BROWSER
The tool is a single HTML file written in plain JavaScript, and it was built that way deliberately: there is no build step for anyone to reproduce and no external library that can change beneath it, so the page should keep working unaltered for as long as the NASA POWER API does.

Reading the result

Soil moisture in central Chile has declined steadily across the full forty-five years of record, and the Mann–Kendall test puts the probability of a pattern this consistent arising in data with no underlying trend at below one in ten thousand.

The seasonal cycle is removed before any test is applied, because it would otherwise dominate the record. In Santiago the difference between summer and winter is around fourteen degrees while the trend is 0.17 °C per decade, so a trend of that size is entirely hidden inside the annual cycle until the cycle is subtracted.

Five further worked examples are built into the tool. Every figure quoted in this presentation was produced by the tool itself from live data, rather than taken from published literature.

Method note — the correction that matters most

Consecutive months are not independent observations.

Because conditions in one month are correlated with those in the month before, a record of 540 monthly values does not carry 540 independent pieces of evidence about the underlying trend. Testing it as though it did understates the standard error, with the result that almost any fluctuation is returned as significant.

The tool therefore estimates the lag-1 autocorrelation of the regression residuals and reduces the sample size to match, following the effective sample size approach of Santer et al. (2000). For Amazon relative humidity this discards most of the apparent evidence before the test is applied:

540 → 76
effective sample size after correction
The Amazon trend remains significant once corrected, which is precisely what the correction is for: it removes the trends that were products of the method rather than of the data. Both r₁ and the corrected sample size are shown on screen, and the tool warns when what remains is too small to carry weight.
Three tests, applied to every series

Least squares, with a t-test

Fits a straight line by minimising squared residuals and reports the rate of change with a 95% confidence interval, on the assumption that the change is linear and the residuals approximately normal.

Mann–Kendall

A rank-based test that assumes nothing about the shape of the trend or the distribution of the data, asking only whether later values tend to exceed earlier ones, which makes it the more dependable test for a skewed variable such as precipitation.

Theil–Sen slope

Estimates the slope as the median of every pairwise slope in the record, so that a small number of extreme months cannot displace the estimate.

Within one region, the trend runs in both directions at once.

88 cells where rainfall is rising, significantly
29 cells where rainfall is falling, significantly
156 cells where the record remains too noisy to call either way
Averaged across the whole box the trend is +0.107 mm/day per decade, and that figure is itself significant, but it describes the behaviour of very few of the cells it was computed from, since it combines strong increases in the west with significant decreases running through the centre and east.
This is the difficulty the challenge takes its name from, and it is the reason the tool tests every grid cell separately instead of returning one regional figure.

Closing note
London rainfall, 1981–2025
p = 0.51
Not significant

Rainfall in London has risen very slightly over forty-five years, but a p-value of 0.51 means a record this noisy would produce a pattern at least this strong about half the time even if nothing were changing at all.

A negative result is still a result, and the tool reports it as one.

A trend may well be present and simply not yet detectable in forty-five years of data, and saying so is more useful than returning a confident answer that a longer record would overturn.

The habits the tool is meant to encourage follow from that: remove the seasonal cycle before looking for a trend, apply more than one test rather than trusting a single p-value, discount observations that are not independent of one another, and accept the answer that comes out.
