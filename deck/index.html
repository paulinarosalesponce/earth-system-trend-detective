Two things stop people from finding real change in them: 1. **Too many variables.** Where do you even start? 2. **Trends disagree with themselves.** The same variable rises in one place and falls a few hundred kilometres away — so one global number hides the story. And a trend is not a result until someone has tested it. *Visual: the East Africa dot map — red and blue cells inside one box.* --- ## Slide 3 — The tool, in three clicks **1. The full statistics underneath. --- ## Slide 5 — The statistics, done honestly Three independent tests on every series: **least squares + t-test** · **Mann–Kendall** · **Theil–Sen slope** **And the correction most tools skip.** A warm month follows a warm month, so 540 monthly values are not 540 independent pieces of evidence. We measure the lag-1 autocorrelation and cut the sample to an effective n — for Amazon humidity, 540 becomes 76 — then test against *that*. The tool shows the discount instead of hiding it. Verified against SciPy and pymannkendall: agreement to 1 × 10⁻⁹. *Visual: the statistics panel with the n_eff note highlighted.* --- ## Slide 6 — The hard case, made visible **East African rainfall, 1981–2025.** One box. 273 NASA grid cells. - **88 cells rising significantly** - **29 cells falling significantly** A single regional average would have called this "no change." Testing every cell separately shows what is really happening — and that is the whole point of the challenge. *Visual: the East Africa map, zoomed, with the cell-count chips.* --- ## Slide 7 — Finding nothing is a finding **London rainfall, 1981–2025: p = 0.51. Not significant.** The tool says so plainly, and explains that some trends only emerge with a longer record. A tool that always finds a trend is not a detective — it is a rubber stamp.
